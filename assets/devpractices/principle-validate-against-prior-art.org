#+title: Validate Design Assumptions Against Prior Art
#+filetags: :principle:process:devpractice:

Before designing a new solution to a problem from scratch, check what existing implementations, libraries, protocols, or systems have already solved a closely related version of it, and use them to validate — or challenge — the assumptions underlying the new design. This is not an argument for always reusing an existing solution wholesale; there are legitimate reasons to build something new. It's an argument for never designing in a vacuum, because a problem that looks novel from the inside of a specific project has very often already been solved, imperfectly or otherwise, by someone else, and their solution encodes hard-won knowledge about the edge cases that make the problem harder than it looks at first pass.

Reinventing a solved problem usually rediscovers the same edge cases the hard way. The pattern is consistent across domains: a team designs a new synchronization protocol, a new caching strategy, a new authentication flow, a new data format, confident that their situation is different enough to warrant a bespoke approach — and months in, they discover, one production incident at a time, the exact set of edge cases that the prior art they didn't study had already encountered and designed around: the concurrent-write conflict that an existing CRDT library resolves cleanly and their bespoke merge logic doesn't; the token-refresh race condition that an established auth protocol's specification explicitly calls out and their custom flow missed entirely; the numeric precision edge case that an existing, boring, well-tested library handles and their from-scratch reimplementation gets wrong on exactly the input nobody thought to test. None of these edge cases were exotic — they were already documented, somewhere, by whoever solved the adjacent problem first. The cost wasn't the existence of the edge case; it was paying full production-incident price to rediscover something that was one search or one design-doc read away from being known upfront.

The corrective is a research step built into the design process, not an optional nicety: before finalizing a novel design for a non-trivial problem, spend real time looking for how others have approached the same or a closely analogous problem — existing libraries even if you don't plan to use them directly, published protocol specifications, postmortems from other teams or projects, academic or industry writeups of the tradeoffs involved. The goal isn't necessarily to adopt the existing solution unmodified; it's to surface the edge cases and tradeoffs it had to navigate, so that if your design diverges from it, the divergence is a deliberate, informed choice rather than an accidental omission born of not knowing the edge case existed.

This is especially important for anything security-, consistency-, or protocol-adjacent, where the "obvious" naive design is disproportionately likely to have a known, named failure mode that prior art already had to solve — rolling your own cryptographic primitive or your own conflict-resolution algorithm without studying the established alternatives is the canonical version of this mistake, precisely because those domains are unusually rich in non-obvious edge cases that took the field years to fully catalogue.

This principle feeds directly into [[id:principle:explicit-design-stances][explicit design stances]] — the output of validating against prior art should be written down as part of the design rationale, including which prior art was considered and why the chosen approach diverges from it where it does — and into [[id:practice:adr-format][ADR format]]'s "alternatives rejected" section, which is exactly where this research belongs once a decision is made.
