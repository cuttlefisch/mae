#+title: Prior-Art Review Before Deciding
#+filetags: :adr:research:devpractice:

Before an architecture decision is written down, review it against published, proven practice — and
review it specifically for *evidence that contradicts the choice you are inclined toward*. An ADR
grounded only in reasoning about the codebase records what the author found plausible; an ADR
grounded in prior art records what the field has already learned, including the costs the author
had not thought of.

This is not literature review for its own sake. It is cheap insurance against a specific, common
failure: a decision that is locally coherent, internally justified, and already known elsewhere to
fail. Most architectural mistakes are not novel.

* When it applies

Reach for this when a decision has any of these properties:

- It establishes a *convention* others will follow, rather than making one local change.
- It concerns security, authorization, concurrency, data integrity, or protocol design — domains
  where the failure modes are subtle and the literature is deep.
- The reasoning behind it is your own judgment rather than a measurement.
- You are about to reject an alternative on intuition ("that would be surprising", "that feels
  like overkill"). A rejected alternative defended by taste is the highest-value thing to check.

Skip it for reversible, local, mechanical changes. The test is whether being wrong would be
expensive to undo.

* The procedure

1. **State the decision and its arguable parts explicitly**, before searching. Write down which
   parts rest on judgment rather than evidence. Those are the research questions. A review that
   starts from "is this good?" returns nothing useful; one that starts from "I rejected X on the
   grounds that Y — is that right?" returns a verdict.

2. **Ask for the refutation, not the confirmation.** Brief the search to find the strongest case
   *against* the intended choice, and to name systems that tried it and retreated. Vendor
   postmortems and deprecation notices are unusually high-value: a feature removed from a shipping
   product is a decision someone paid for.

3. **Prefer primary sources.** Specifications, papers, official documentation, changelogs, and
   incident writeups over summaries and blog commentary. Quote the load-bearing sentence rather
   than paraphrasing it — paraphrase is where the meaning quietly shifts to match what you wanted
   it to say.

4. **Demand an explicit verdict per question** — supports / complicates / contradicts. A summary
   of "what's out there" is not a decision input. A verdict is.

5. **Verify the claims that carry the decision.** Both the external ones (does the cited source
   actually say that?) and the internal ones (does our code actually do what the decision assumes?).
   Research is generated text like any other; a citation that cannot be checked should not be
   load-bearing.

6. **Record the outcome in the ADR** — an Evidence section listing what was consulted and what it
   established. Include the sources that *cut against* the decision. An ADR whose evidence section
   is unanimously supportive is usually one where the search was aimed at confirmation.

* What to do when the evidence contradicts you

Change the decision, and say so in the ADR rather than quietly editing it into agreement. A
revision note naming the reversed decision and the evidence that reversed it is more useful to a
future reader than a document that looks like it was right the first time — it tells them the
decision was genuinely tested, and it shows them what kind of argument is strong enough to move it.

If the evidence is mixed, say that too, and record which way the residual risk points.

* Failure modes

- **Confirmation search.** Asking "why is X good?" and finding out. The brief must invite refutation
  explicitly, or the answer is worthless.
- **Cargo-culting scale.** A practice that is correct for a system with thousands of contributors
  or millions of users may be pure overhead at a smaller scale. Prior art tells you what a design
  costs and where it breaks — it does not tell you that you have the same problem. Check that the
  cited system's constraints resemble yours before adopting its answer.
- **Citation laundering.** A plausible-sounding attribution to a famous paper that the paper does
  not contain. Verify quotes against the source text; this failure is common and hard to spot,
  because the fabricated claim is usually one the field would agree with anyway.
- **Deciding by consensus count.** "Five systems do X" is weaker than one well-documented account
  of *why* X, and much weaker than a documented case of X failing. Weigh reasons, not votes.

* Relation to other practices

This sits upstream of [[id:practice:adr-format][ADR Format]]: the review happens before the record
is written, and its output becomes the Evidence section and sharpens Alternatives Rejected. It is
the design-time counterpart of [[id:practice:adversarial-testing][Adversarial Testing]] — the same
instinct (try to falsify it) applied to a decision rather than to an implementation, and for the
same reason: a conclusion is trustworthy in proportion to how hard you tried to break it.
