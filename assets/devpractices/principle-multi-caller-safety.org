#+title: Design Services for Multiple Simultaneous Callers
#+filetags: :principle:concurrency:devpractice:

A service or API that may have multiple simultaneous callers — multiple human sessions, multiple AI agents, or a mix of both — must never assume it is the only caller. This is distinct from general concurrency safety: it isn't just about protecting a single piece of mutable state from a race, it's about the specific shape of one server or process fanning out to N independent callers, none of which can be trusted to behave, none of which should be able to degrade service for the others, and any of which might disappear or stall mid-interaction without warning.

Three concrete design commitments follow from taking this shape seriously. First, bound the resources any one caller can consume: a per-caller queue with a fixed capacity, rather than an unbounded one, means a single slow or misbehaving caller fills its own queue and stops there, instead of growing without limit and eventually exhausting memory shared by every other caller too. Second, decide explicitly what happens when a caller can't keep up — drop that caller's queued updates and let it catch up on the next poll, or enforce a write timeout that disconnects a caller which isn't reading fast enough — rather than blocking the whole system waiting for one caller to catch up, or letting its backlog grow forever. Third, any state mutation must be safe for concurrent observation by callers that didn't initiate it: a change one caller makes needs to be visible to, and not corrupt the view of, every other connected caller, including ones that were mid-read when the mutation happened.

The failure mode when this is ignored: a service is built and tested against a single client, so every design decision implicitly assumes that client's behavior is representative — a queue that grows without bound because the one test client always drains it promptly, a state mutation that isn't synchronized because the one test client never reads concurrently with a write. The service ships, a second client connects, and now one of two things happens: either the second client's slow reads back up an unbounded queue until the process runs out of memory and the first client's connection dies with it, or the two clients observe torn, half-written state because nothing ever enforced consistency across concurrent observers. Neither failure was visible in single-client testing, because single-client testing structurally cannot exercise the multi-caller interaction that caused it — it needed a second, independent, possibly slow or misbehaving caller in the room to show up.

This matters more, not less, as AI-assisted development becomes normal: a human operating a system interactively and one or more AI agents also acting on that same system, concurrently, is an increasingly ordinary situation rather than a hypothetical edge case reserved for large multi-tenant services. A service built assuming one interactive caller will be handed a second, machine-speed caller sooner than its authors expect, and machine-speed callers are exactly the ones most likely to expose an unbounded queue or an unsynchronized mutation, because they can generate load and concurrent access patterns a human never would.

This is the specific N-caller-fanout shape of the broader [[id:principle:concurrency-safety-first][concurrency-safety-first]] principle — everything that principle says about designing shared-access rules up front applies here, narrowed to the specific case of one service serving many independent, untrusted, differently-paced callers. It also connects directly to [[id:principle:ai-as-peer-actor][treating an AI agent as a peer actor]]: an AI agent calling into a shared service is a caller with exactly the same standing as a human caller, which is precisely why this shape is now common rather than a corner case. [[id:practice:tiered-agent-permissions][Tiered agent permissions]] is a concrete instance of this concern applied specifically to what an AI caller is allowed to do, not just how it's scheduled alongside other callers.
